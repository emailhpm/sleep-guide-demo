        How sleep works

        Your internal clock, sleep pressure, and the stages of a night.

        Sleep isn’t something you can force. It’s the result of multiple systems working together, responding to the signals you send throughout the day.

        Two processes at the same time

        The first is your circadian rhythm, often called your internal clock. It responds mostly to light and darkness, telling your body when to feel alert and when to prepare for rest. Morning light helps set this rhythm, while late-night light can delay it.

        The second is sleep pressure. The longer you’re awake, the more sleep pressure builds. If you nap too long, lie in bed awake, or stay inactive all day, this pressure doesn’t build properly.

        The stages of a night

        1. N1

          Drifting off

          That in-between feeling when you’re no longer fully awake? This is the first, lightest stage of sleep.

        2. N2

          Settling into sleep

          You’re now asleep, though not yet in the deepest stage. It’s another part of the cycle your body returns to during the night.

        3. N3

          Deep sleep

          Also called slow-wave sleep, this is your deepest non-REM stage. More of it usually happens earlier in the night.

        4. REM

          When dreams get vivid

          Your brain becomes more active, and vivid dreams often happen here. REM periods tend to be longer later in the night.

        The cycle repeats through the night.

        REM and deep sleep: why both matter

        Deep sleep is the stage of physical repair and recovery. REM sleep is where the brain does much of its memory work, learning and emotional processing.

        Both are sensitive to stress, the way you eat, and irregular schedules. That is why the rest of this library looks at your whole day, not just bedtime.

        Light & screens

        Morning light, evening light, and what screens add to the picture.

        Light is the strongest signal your body uses to decide when to feel awake and when to prepare for sleep. Screens matter, but they are only one part of a much bigger picture. What you do with light throughout the entire day counts for more than the final hour before bed.

        Morning light vs evening light

        Morning light helps anchor your circadian rhythm and tells your brain that the day has started. This early signal supports natural melatonin production later in the evening.

        Evening light sends the opposite message. Bright or overhead lighting late at night can delay sleep signals and keep the brain in alert mode longer than intended. Over time, this mismatch can quietly shift your sleep rhythm later and later.

        Timing matters more than darkness alone

        Sleep is not just about darkness. It is about when your body receives light cues. A well-timed morning light signal can make the body more tolerant to small amounts of evening light. Consistency and timing matter more than perfect darkness.

        How screen light can delay sleep signals

        Delayed signals. Bright screens in the evening keep sending daytime light cues, so melatonin release may be pushed later.

        Mental stimulation. Gripping or emotionally charged content can keep the brain active long after the screen is put away.

        An alert nervous system. Constant input, notifications and rapid visual changes keep the nervous system in a more activated state.

        A blurred wind-down. Late screen use blurs the transition between daytime activity and rest, and the body receives mixed signals.

        From the guide's 7-day reset: get 10 to 30 minutes of natural light within the first hour after waking, outside or by a bright window, and dim the lights in the evening.

        Food & sleep

        Steady meals, a balanced evening plate, and five foods from the guide.

        Nutrition plays a quiet but important role in sleep. What and when you eat sends signals about energy availability and safety, helping the body decide whether it is time to stay alert or rest.

        Steady meals

        Long gaps between meals, very light dinners, or restrictive eating can trigger stress hormones overnight, pulling the body out of sleep. Regular meals and a balanced evening intake keep blood sugar steadier through the night.

        Protein and carbs in the evening

        Protein provides the building blocks for neurotransmitters involved in sleep and relaxation. Carbohydrates, when paired with protein, can support calming sleep signals in the evening. Nutrients such as magnesium, glycine and tryptophan are often best obtained through food.

        Five foods from the guide

        1. Salmon

          Protein, omega-3 fats and vitamin B6.

        2. Oats

          Complex carbohydrates for steady blood sugar overnight, plus small amounts of magnesium.

        3. Greek yogurt

          Protein and calcium. Choose plain, unsweetened yogurt.

        4. Bananas

          Magnesium, potassium and carbohydrates. A gentle part of a balanced evening snack.

        5. Pumpkin seeds

          Magnesium, zinc and tryptophan. A small portion in the evening.

        What supports, what disrupts

        Sleep-supportive: protein-rich foods (fish, yogurt, eggs, tofu), complex carbohydrates (oats, quinoa, brown rice), magnesium-rich foods (pumpkin seeds, leafy greens, nuts), calcium-containing foods (yogurt, tahini, fortified alternatives), and balanced evening meals or light snacks combining protein and carbs.

        Sleep-disrupting: heavy, processed, or sugary meals late in the day can increase alertness and disrupt overnight sleep.

        When to drink

        Drink earlier and more evenly, not more at night.

        Hydration affects sleep more than most people realize. Both dehydration and poor electrolyte balance can contribute to night waking and restlessness. The goal is not to drink more at night, but to hydrate earlier and more evenly during the day.

        Even mild dehydration can increase nighttime awakenings. When fluid levels are low, the body may trigger thirst signals that interrupt sleep. Many people who wake frequently at night are actually under-hydrated during the day.

        Hydration is not just about water. Electrolytes such as sodium, potassium and magnesium help regulate fluid balance, muscle relaxation and nervous system function.

        Your hydration timing

        1. Morning

          Start early

          Start hydrating early after waking and include fluids with breakfast.

        2. Midday

          The main share

          Aim for the majority of your daily fluids. Include electrolytes if you are active or stressed.

        3. Afternoon

          Keep it steady

          Continue steady hydration and begin tapering large amounts of fluid.

        4. Evening

          Small sips

          Small sips only if needed. Avoid large drinks 1 to 2 hours before bed.

        Movement

        Move often, keep it calm, and mind the timing.

        Movement plays an important role in sleep, but more is not always better. The type, intensity, and timing of movement all influence how the nervous system responds at night.

        Move daily, even lightly

        Regular movement supports sleep more than occasional intense workouts. Even light daily movement helps regulate stress hormones and build healthy sleep pressure. The goal is to move often, not exhaust the body.

        Choose calming movement

        Choose movement that leaves you feeling calmer afterward. When exercise is overly intense or stressful, it can keep the body in alert mode into the evening.

        Timing and recovery

        Earlier movement in the day helps reinforce natural sleep–wake rhythms. Late, intense workouts may delay relaxation and make it harder to wind down. Rest days and lighter movement are part of the process.

        The guide's best movement types

        1. Walking

          Supports circulation and stress reduction without overstimulating the nervous system. Regular walks, especially earlier in the day, help reinforce natural daily rhythms.

        2. Light to moderate strength

          Supports muscle recovery without excessive nervous system activation, done earlier in the day or late afternoon.

        3. Structured strength

          Fits when recovery is adequate and sessions are well timed. When sleep is fragile, reduce the intensity or shift it earlier in the day.

        4. Mind-body practices

          Combine movement with controlled breathing to calm the nervous system. Slower, restorative styles suit sleep more than fast-paced or high-intensity versions.

        Movement is one signal your body uses to decide when it’s safe to rest. Light, food timing, stress, and recovery all work together.

        Calm your nervous system

        Five minutes that signal safety, even on busy or low-energy days.

        Many sleep issues are not caused by a lack of sleep knowledge, but by a nervous system that never fully shifts into rest. A tired body does not always mean a calm nervous system, especially in times of ongoing stress or overload.

        Even when the body feels exhausted, the nervous system may remain alert, making it difficult to relax. It settles when it receives consistent signals of safety throughout the day, not just at bedtime.

        5-minute reset options

        These options are designed to fit into real life. Choose one time block and stop when the time is up, no need to do everything.

        1. 1 minute

          Breathing reset

          Breathe slowly through the nose, letting the exhale be slightly longer than the inhale. One focused minute is enough.

        2. 1 to 2 minutes

          Gentle movement

          Stand up and move slowly through light walking, stretching, or shaking out tension. Keep movements smooth and unforced.

        3. 1 to 2 minutes

          Sensory grounding

          Place your feet on the floor and notice physical sensations like pressure, temperature, or texture. This is especially useful when the mind feels busy.

        4. 1 minute

          Visual calm

          Rest your eyes on something steady and calming, such as a window, plant, or dim light. Let your gaze soften rather than focus.

        5. 30 seconds

          Predictable pause

          Pause at the same time each day, even briefly. Consistency matters more than duration.

        If slow breathing feels activating, choose movement or sensory grounding instead.

        Your morning & evening

        Morning habits set your rhythm. Evening routines signal rest.

        Sleep is shaped by what happens across the entire day, not just at bedtime. Morning habits help set your body’s rhythm, while evening routines help signal safety and prepare the nervous system for rest.

        Morning: set the rhythm

        Wake at a consistent time

        Waking at a similar time daily helps regulate your sleep rhythm, even after a poor night.

        Get morning light

        Natural light within the first hour of waking supports alertness and evening melatonin.

        Move gently

        Light movement such as walking or stretching helps wake the body without overstimulation.

        Eat a balanced first meal

        Protein and carbohydrates help stabilize blood sugar and reduce stress hormone spikes later in the day.

        Hydrate early

        Drinking fluids earlier supports energy and reduces nighttime thirst.

        Evening: signal rest

        Dim the lights

        Lower lighting in the evening helps signal that the day is ending.

        Eat dinner earlier

        Balanced meals eaten 2 to 3 hours before bed support digestion.

        Slow movement only

        Gentle stretching or relaxed movement helps release tension without activating the nervous system.

        Create a wind-down window

        Repeating calming activities nightly helps the body associate these cues with sleep.

        Reduce screen stimulation

        Lower brightness and avoid stimulating content close to bedtime when possible.

        The 7-day reset

        One focus per day. You don’t need to do everything perfectly.

        Focus on one day at a time and keep the changes from the days before.

        1. Day 1

          Set your wake time

          Do this: Choose a wake time and keep it the same today and tomorrow, even after a poor night.

          Adjust if needed: Go to bed earlier or rest during the day, but don’t sleep in.

        2. Day 2

          Get morning light

          Do this: Get 10 to 30 minutes of natural light within the first hour after waking, outside or by a bright window.

          Adjust if needed: Combine light with a short walk or breakfast near a window.

        3. Day 3

          Shift caffeine earlier

          Do this: Keep coffee and other caffeine to the earlier part of the day.

          Adjust if needed: Reduce slowly instead of cutting it completely.

        4. Day 4

          Support evening blood sugar

          Do this: Eat dinner 2 to 3 hours before bed, with protein, carbs, and fat.

          Adjust if needed: Add a small, balanced evening snack if you wake hungry.

        5. Day 5

          Move your body

          Do this: Do 20 to 40 minutes of walking, stretching, or light strength training earlier in the day. It builds sleep pressure.

          Adjust if needed: Keep movement gentle if intense workouts disrupt your sleep.

        6. Day 6

          Create a wind-down cue

          Do this: Repeat the same calming activity every night, such as stretching, reading, or dim lights. It signals safety to the nervous system.

          Adjust if needed: Keep it short. Consistency matters more than length.

        7. Day 7

          Regulate during the day

          Do this: Take 1 to 2 short regulation breaks: slow breathing, grounding, or light movement.

          Adjust if needed: Skip breathwork if it feels activating, and choose movement or sensory grounding instead.

        Some nights will feel easier than others. That is part of how the body adjusts.
